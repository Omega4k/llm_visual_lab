A model never sees words. It sees tokens: small chunks of text. Type below and watch your sentence break into chunks. Common words stay whole; long or rare words split into pieces.

Medium · goes deeperThose chunks come from a fixed vocabulary, produced by a tokenizer before the model itself sees anything. Spaces and punctuation count as parts of tokens too — try adding punctuation, a capital, or an extra space in the text above and watch the count change.

Expert · goes deeperMore precisely, the splitting rule is learned, not hand-written: a subword tokenizer (BPE-style merges over bytes) segments text into IDs from that fixed vocabulary. That is why token count — not character count — drives cost and context usage, and why models can be blind to individual letters inside a token. 2 · Embeddings — words as places

Inside the model, every word becomes a list of numbers — a point in space. Words that mean similar things sit close together. Drag to look around the cloud, scroll to zoom, and find the animal, vehicle, and food groups.

The 3D view needs an internet connection for its library on first load. The idea: each labelled point is a word, and distance means similarity.

Try this: find the two clusters. Why do you think king and queen sit apart from car and bus, even though all four are nouns?

Why are king and queen close together?

Nobody told the model they are related. During training (next lesson) it read billions of sentences and kept adjusting these positions so it could guess masked or next words better. King and queen show up in almost interchangeable sentences — “the ___ wore a crown”, “the ___ ruled the kingdom”, “long live the ___” — so the positions that best predict those shared contexts end up nearly identical. Car and bus share a different set of contexts (“parked the ___”, “drove the ___”), so they form their own cluster far away. Closeness in this space literally means “used in similar sentences.” That is also why the geometry carries meaning: the direction from man to king is roughly the same direction as from woman to queen — royalty, learned from usage alone.

Medium · goes deeperThat list of numbers has a name: an embedding — a vector learned during training, not designed by anyone. Because words used in similar sentences get similar vectors, meaning shows up as distance in the cloud above: near means “used alike”, far means “used differently”.

Expert · goes deeperFormally, each token ID indexes a row of the embedding matrix E ∈ ℝV×d, where d is in the thousands in a real model; the cloud above projects a toy version down to 3D. Cosine proximity encodes distributional similarity, and directions can carry relational structure — the classic example is king − man + woman ≈ queen.

3 · Training — how it learned all this

Before the model could answer anything, it practised — a lot. It read text, guessed the next word, checked, and fixed its mistakes by a tiny amount. After billions of tries, words used in similar ways ended up as neighbours. Press Train and watch eight words organise themselves as the error score falls.

Loss: 100%

Training loss — lower is better

The loop, as maths — gradient descent:
1 · Guess: the model outputs probabilities p for the next token.
2 · Measure: loss = −log p(correct token) — huge when it was confidently wrong, near zero when right.
3 · Blame: each weight gets a gradient ∂loss/∂w — its personal share of the blame (the chain rule carries the error backwards, layer by layer: “backpropagation”).
4 · Nudge: w ← w − η · ∂loss/∂w, with learning rate η small (the toy model in lesson 7 uses η = 0.4) so it improves instead of jumping past the answer.
Repeat billions of times and the loss landscape is slowly descended — like walking downhill in fog, always stepping along the steepest downward slope you can feel underfoot.

Toy model, real principle. The real thing trains on trillions of tokens for weeks on thousands of processors, and the “words” are tokens in a space with thousands of dimensions — but the loop is the same: guess, measure the error, nudge, repeat. Crucially, training is finished before you ever type a prompt; chatting with the model (inference) changes nothing inside it. It is not learning from you.

The loss landscape — gradient descent in 3D

Imagine every possible setting of the model as a place in a hilly landscape, where the height is how wrong it is. Training is walking downhill to the lowest point. The ball below can only feel the slope under its feet — that slope is the gradient — and each step goes downhill by an amount set by the learning rate. Too small: it crawls. Too big: it leaps across the valley and climbs the other side.

Press “Take one step” to see the gradient maths with real numbers.

Medium · goes deeperThe loop above has names: predict the next token, measure the error (loss), then adjust every weight slightly downhill — gradient descent, written w ← w − η·∇L. Embeddings organise because words in similar contexts receive similar nudges. For the landscape: try η = 0.1 (crawls), 0.45 (smooth), then 1.1 (overshoots and oscillates).

Expert · goes deeperPre-training minimises next-token cross-entropy over trillions of tokens by stochastic gradient descent, with the error carried back through every layer (backpropagation). The toys above use the same update, w ← w − η·∂L/∂w. The landscape surface is L(x,y) = 0.6x² + 0.25y² + 0.35·sin(x)·cos(y) — a non-convex bowl standing in for a surface over billions of weights. Watch ∇L shrink near the minimum; reset with η = 1.2 and the ball leaps across it and has to come back — on a worse-conditioned surface, that is where divergence begins.

4 · Attention — deciding what matters

Words can be ambiguous — in “The cat sat on the mat because it was tired,” what does it mean? Attention lets each word look back at the words before it and decide which ones matter. Click a word to see where it looks, and try the different heads.

Schematic teaching data, simplified from how real heads behave. A real model has dozens of layers, each with many heads, all voting at once — and tokens can only look backwards, never ahead.

Medium · goes deeperLooking back is only half of it: each token mixes the information from the tokens it weighted into its own representation, so its meaning is rewritten by its context. The heads run in parallel and specialise — one tracks reference (what “it” means), one tracks verbs, one tracks the previous word. Switch heads above and compare what changes.

Expert · goes deeperThe mechanism is scaled dot-product attention: softmax(QKT/√d)V, computed under a causal mask so each position attends only backwards. Each head projects Q, K and V differently per layer, which is why heads specialise (induction, coreference, syntax). The weights in the demo above are schematic; the mixing operation is the real one.

5 · Next token — a weighted coin flip

The model doesn't pick the next word outright. It gives every possible word a score, turns scores into odds, and rolls a weighted dice. Press Step to pick one word at a time. The temperature slider makes the dice fair (wild) or loaded (safe).

The capital of France is

Candidate odds are teaching data with realistic shape; the temperature maths applied to them is the real maths.

Medium · goes deeperThose scores have a name: logits — one per token, output by the final layer. A function called softmax converts them into probabilities that sum to 1, and picking a word is sampling from that distribution. Temperature rescales the logits before softmax: below 1 sharpens the odds, above 1 flattens them. Move the slider above, then Step, and watch both happen.

Expert · goes deeperFormally, p = softmax(z/T) over the full vocabulary — about 128k tokens in the local Llama; the bars above show a teaching subset. After sampling, the chosen token is appended to the input and the whole forward pass repeats: that loop is what autoregressive means. T→0 approaches argmax (greedy decoding), T>1 increases entropy. The candidate odds here are didactic; the temperature transform applied to them is exact.

6 · Inside the model — journey of a token

Inside the model, your words travel through a stack of layers, like floors in a building. On every floor, each word listens to the others and then thinks about what it now knows. Pick a word and press play to ride along as it changes, floor by floor, until it becomes an answer.

Schematic teaching model — the real model stacks layers like this sketch, but the Ollama API does not expose its real internal numbers, so these values are illustrative, shaped like real behaviour: early layers handle position and neighbours, middle layers resolve references like “it”, late layers commit to an answer. For a model's true insides, see the next lesson — a tiny model that really trains in this page.

Medium · goes deeperThere is no single path through a transformer: every token passes through every layer. Each layer runs attention (mixing between tokens) then an MLP (processing within a token) — two different jobs, one rewriting what a token knows about its neighbours, the other about itself. That pair is what rewrites the token's representation as it rises.

Expert · goes deeperA forward pass is a residual stream: xl+1 = xl + Attn(xl) + MLP(xl + Attn), per layer, with all positions processed in parallel. Early layers mostly encode position and syntax, middle layers resolve reference, and late layers align the stream with the unembedding so the final logits favour the answer. The bars above show 16 of the thousands of numbers in that stream.

The maths, with this model's real numbers

Train or pick a word to see its numbers.

Reading it: each hidden node computes h = tanh(Σ w·x + b), each output word gets a score z = Σ h·W + b, and softmax turns scores into probabilities that add to 100%: p = ez / Σ ez. During training, the error for the correct word is loss = −log p(correct), and gradient descent nudges every weight against its gradient: w ← w − η · ∂loss/∂w, where the output gradient has the beautifully simple form p − 1 for the correct word and p for every wrong one — push down what it wrongly believed, push up what it missed, a fraction η at a time.

Train it on your own sentences

Replace its whole world: write a few short, repetitive sentences (one per line, at least two words each). The model rebuilds its vocabulary from your words and retrains from scratch. Patterns you repeat are the patterns it will learn.

Try this: before training, pick “cat” — the prediction is near-random.

Train, then look again: after “cat” it should strongly favour “sat”, and after “the” it should spread across “cat”, “dog” and “bird” — because that is exactly the pattern in its tiny world. You are watching lesson 3 (training) create lesson 5 (prediction), with nothing hidden.

Medium · goes deeperInside, each word goes in as numbers, passes through two hidden layers of 16 and 12 nodes, and comes out as probabilities for the next word. Node size and brightness in the 3D view above are real activations, and the amber lines trace the strongest connections behind the prediction — nothing is staged.

Expert · goes deeperThe network, exactly: one-hot input → 16 tanh units → 12 tanh units → softmax over a 12-word vocabulary, trained by SGD on next-word pairs. Unlike the Llama in lesson 10, every weight and activation lives in this page, so the amber route can show the largest |h·W| contributions to the winning logit. It is an MLP, not a transformer — no attention — which is what makes total inspection possible.

8 · Context — why it forgets

The model can only remember a limited amount of your conversation. When too much has been said, the oldest words fall away and are forgotten — it can't miss them, because for the model they no longer exist. Add sentences and watch the beginning disappear.

0 / 18 tokens in window · 0 tokens forgotten

Context window fill

Real windows are far bigger (this small model advertises up to 128k tokens), but the rule is identical at any size. This is also why long chats “drift”: the instruction you gave at the start may literally have left the window.

Medium · goes deeperThat limited amount has a name: the context window — a fixed number of recent tokens. Your prompts and the model's answers all live in it together; past the limit, the oldest tokens drop off the front, exactly as in the demo above.

Expert · goes deeperInference is stateless: the entire state is the token sequence inside that fixed window (the local model advertises 128k tokens; the toy window above holds 18). Tokens beyond the limit are not compressed or summarised — they are absent from the next forward pass, which is why long sessions silently lose early instructions.

9 · Limits — confident, plausible, wrong

The model tries to say things that sound right — which is not the same as being right. When it doesn't know, it often makes things up, confidently. Each button below loads a trap question into the live model; guess what will happen before you run it.

Why it fails: counting needs letters, but the model sees tokens (lesson 1); citations are easy to imitate without existing; and the maths answer that “sounds right” (10 cents) out-competes the correct one (5 cents) unless the model slows down. Bigger models fall for these less — none are immune.

Medium · goes deeperThe reason is in the training: it optimises for plausible text, not verified truth. With no fact database and no self-check, gaps get filled fluently — that failure mode is called hallucination. Small models do it constantly; watch for it when the traps run in lesson 10.

Expert · goes deeperThe objective is likelihood, not truth: nothing in next-token training grounds a claim, and decoding will happily continue a false premise in perfect style. Expect fabricated citations, tokenisation-driven counting failures, and high-probability wrong answers (the 10¢ bat-and-ball lure). Larger models reduce all three — they eliminate none.
