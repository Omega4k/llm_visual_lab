1 · Tokens — how the model reads

One idea carries the whole course: a language model is a prediction machine, not a database. It doesn't look answers up; it guesses what comes next, one small piece at a time, astonishingly well. Everything else — the cleverness, the mistakes, the confident made-up facts in lesson 10 — follows from that. And it all starts with how the model reads, because it doesn't read the way you do.

A model never sees words. It sees tokens: small chunks of text. Type below and watch your sentence break into chunks. Common words stay whole; long or rare words split into pieces.

2 · Embeddings — words as places

Inside the model, every word becomes a list of numbers — a point in space. Words that mean similar things sit close together. Drag to look around the cloud, scroll to zoom, and find the animal, vehicle, and food groups.

The 3D view needs an internet connection for its library on first load. The idea: each labelled point is a word, and distance means similarity.

Try this: find the two clusters. Why do you think king and queen sit apart from car and bus, even though all four are nouns?

Why are king and queen close together?

Nobody told the model they are related. During training (next lesson) it read billions of sentences and kept adjusting these positions so it could guess masked or next words better. King and queen show up in almost interchangeable sentences — “the ___ wore a crown”, “the ___ ruled the kingdom”, “long live the ___” — so the positions that best predict those shared contexts end up nearly identical. Car and bus share a different set of contexts (“parked the ___”, “drove the ___”), so they form their own cluster far away. Closeness in this space literally means “used in similar sentences.” That is also why the geometry carries meaning: the direction from man to king is roughly the same direction as from woman to queen — royalty, learned from usage alone.

How embeddings are actually stored

The 3D cloud is a flattened picture, not the storage itself. Each embedding starts as a random list of numbers and is a weight like any other: every time its token appears in training, gradient descent (next lesson) nudges that list a little, and tokens used in similar sentences receive similar nudges, so the clusters above organise themselves. What is actually stored is a table — one row of numbers per token, looked up by token ID. In the small Llama used later in this lab that table holds about 128,000 tokens, each a list of 2,048 numbers — thousands of dimensions, not three. The cloud squashes those lists down to 3 axes so you can look at them; the closeness is real (measured by the angle between the full lists), the 3D positions are only an approximation. One more subtlety: the stored row for bank is the same in every sentence — the layers in Lesson 6 rewrite it using context, so “river bank” and “money bank” end up different inside the model even though they started from the same row.

MediumThat list of numbers has a name: an embedding — a vector learned during training, not designed by anyone. Because words used in similar sentences get similar vectors, meaning shows up as distance in the cloud above: near means “used alike”, far means “used differently”.

ExpertFormally, each token ID indexes a row of the embedding matrix E ∈ ℝV×d, where d is in the thousands in a real model; the cloud above projects a toy version down to 3D. Cosine proximity encodes distributional similarity, and directions can carry relational structure — the classic example is king − man + woman ≈ queen.

3 · Training — how it learned all this

Before the model could answer anything, it practised — a lot. It read text, guessed the next word, checked, and fixed its mistakes by a tiny amount. After billions of tries, words used in similar ways ended up as neighbours. Press Train and watch eight words organise themselves as the error score falls.

Loss: 100%

Training loss — lower is better

The loop, as maths — gradient descent:
1 · Guess: the model outputs probabilities p for the next token.
2 · Measure: loss = −log p(correct token) — huge when it was confidently wrong, near zero when right.
3 · Blame: each weight gets a gradient ∂loss/∂w — its personal share of the blame (the chain rule carries the error backwards, layer by layer: “backpropagation”).
4 · Nudge: w ← w − η · ∂loss/∂w, with learning rate η small (the toy model in lesson 8 uses η = 0.4) so it improves instead of jumping past the answer.
Repeat billions of times and the loss landscape is slowly descended — like walking downhill in fog, always stepping along the steepest downward slope you can feel underfoot.

Toy model, real principle. The real thing trains on trillions of tokens for weeks on thousands of processors, and the “words” are tokens in a space with thousands of dimensions — but the loop is the same: guess, measure the error, nudge, repeat. Crucially, training is finished before you ever type a prompt; chatting with the model (inference) changes nothing inside it. It is not learning from you.

Using a model does not retrain it

Gradient descent needs a known correct answer to measure the loss against, and that only exists in training data. While you are chatting with a finished model — inference — the weights are frozen: there is no loss, no gradient, no backward pass and no nudging. The model only runs forwards — read the prompt, predict the next token, add it, repeat (Lesson 5). If a conversation seems to teach it something, that is the context window at work (Lesson 9): the words sit in its short-term memory and shape the next prediction, but no weight changes, and it is all forgotten when the context ends. Teaching a model something permanently always means another training run, done offline by its makers — never the chat itself.

The loss landscape — gradient descent in 3D

Imagine every possible setting of the model as a place in a hilly landscape, where the height is how wrong it is. This landscape has three dips: two shallow ones and one deeper one. Training is walking downhill — but the ball can only feel the slope under its feet, that slope is the gradient, and it has no map. Which dip it ends up in depends on where it starts. Each step goes downhill by an amount set by the learning rate: too small, it crawls; too big, it leaps across the dip and climbs the other side. Try the three start buttons and watch the same rules land in different dips.

MediumThe loop above has names: predict the next token, measure the error (loss), then adjust every weight slightly downhill — gradient descent, written w ← w − η·∇L. Embeddings organise because words in similar contexts receive similar nudges. For the landscape: each dip is a local minimum — downhill in every direction, but only the deepest (east dip, loss ≈ 0.83) is the global minimum. The ball cannot know which it found; gradient ≈ 0 feels identical in all three. Try η = 0.1 (crawls), 0.35 (settles), then 0.8 (too big for these dips — it rattles around or escapes).

ExpertPre-training minimises next-token cross-entropy over trillions of tokens by stochastic gradient descent, with the error carried back through every layer (backpropagation). The toys above use the same update, w ← w − η·∂L/∂w — and the starting point chooses the outcome, because this surface is non-convex: which basin of attraction you start in decides which minimum you converge to. Real training fights this with random restarts of a sort — shuffled data order, noise in every stochastic gradient, momentum — so the optimiser can rattle out of shallow, sharp basins and prefer wide, flat ones that generalise better.

Expert · the landscape, mathematicallyThe surface above is L(x,y) = 0.22(x²+y²) + 3 − Σᵢ dᵢ·exp(−((x−aᵢ)²+(y−bᵢ)²)/(2σᵢ²)): a gentle bowl minus three Gaussian wells at (−1.9, 1.3), (0.2, −1.7) and (2.0, 0.9). Its gradient is exact, not measured: ∂L/∂x = 0.44x + Σᵢ dᵢ·exp(·)·(x−aᵢ)/σᵢ² (and likewise for y) — each well pulls the ball toward its centre with force proportional to distance, fading with the Gaussian. Setting ∇L = 0 gives the stationary points; solving numerically, the minima are (−1.68, 1.15) with L = 1.43, (0.19, −1.51) with L = 1.36, and the global minimum (1.76, 0.79) with L = 0.83. Between every pair of basins sits a saddle ridge: cross it and a different minimum owns you. Curvature decides step size: at the minima the Hessian's largest eigenvalue λmax ≈ 3.5–4.2, and plain gradient descent is only stable for η < 2/λmax ≈ 0.5 — which is why η = 0.45 settles (just barely, with a wobble) and η = 0.8 cannot sit still in any dip. Near a minimum the loss is locally quadratic, L ≈ L* + ½(w−w*)ᵀH(w−w*), so convergence is geometric: the error shrinks by roughly |1 − ηλ| per step along each eigendirection. One honest caveat: in the billions-dimensional real thing, true local minima matter less than this picture suggests — saddle points and connected low-loss valleys dominate high-dimensional geometry, and stochastic gradients rarely sit still long enough to be trapped. The 2D lesson that survives intact is the stability limit and the basin idea, not the fear of dips.

4 · Attention — deciding what matters

Words can be ambiguous — in “The cat sat on the mat because it was tired,” what does it mean? Attention lets each word look back at the words before it and decide which ones matter. Click a word to see where it looks, and try the different heads.

Schematic teaching data, simplified from how real heads behave. A real model has dozens of layers, each with many heads, all voting at once — and tokens can only look backwards, never ahead.

MediumLooking back is only half of it: each token mixes the information from the tokens it weighted into its own representation, so its meaning is rewritten by its context. The heads run in parallel and specialise — one tracks reference (what “it” means), one tracks verbs, one tracks the previous word. Switch heads above and compare what changes.

ExpertThe mechanism is scaled dot-product attention: softmax(QKT/√d)V, computed under a causal mask so each position attends only backwards. Each head projects Q, K and V differently per layer, which is why heads specialise (induction, coreference, syntax). The weights in the demo above are schematic; the mixing operation is the real one.

5 · Next token — a weighted coin flip

The model doesn't pick the next word outright. It gives every possible word a score, turns scores into odds, and rolls a weighted dice. Press Step to pick one word at a time. The temperature slider makes the dice fair (wild) or loaded (safe).

The capital of France is

Candidate odds are teaching data with realistic shape; the temperature maths applied to them is the real maths.

MediumThose scores have a name: logits — one per token, output by the final layer. A function called softmax converts them into probabilities that sum to 1, and picking a word is sampling from that distribution. Temperature rescales the logits before softmax: below 1 sharpens the odds, above 1 flattens them. Move the slider above, then Step, and watch both happen.

ExpertFormally, p = softmax(z/T) over the full vocabulary — about 128k tokens in the local Llama; the bars above show a teaching subset. After sampling, the chosen token is appended to the input and the whole forward pass repeats: that loop is what autoregressive means. T→0 approaches argmax (greedy decoding), T>1 increases entropy. The candidate odds here are didactic; the temperature transform applied to them is exact.

6 · Inside the model — journey of a token

Inside the model, your words travel through a stack of layers, like floors in a building. On every floor, each word listens to the others and then thinks about what it now knows. Pick a word and press play to ride along as it changes, floor by floor, until it becomes an answer.

Schematic teaching model — the real model stacks layers like this sketch, but the Ollama API does not expose its real internal numbers, so these values are illustrative, shaped like real behaviour: early layers handle position and neighbours, middle layers resolve references like “it”, late layers commit to an answer. For a model's true insides, see the next lesson — a tiny model that really trains in this page.

MediumThere is no single path through a transformer: every token passes through every layer. Each layer runs attention (mixing between tokens) then an MLP (processing within a token) — two different jobs, one rewriting what a token knows about its neighbours, the other about itself. That pair is what rewrites the token's representation as it rises.

ExpertA forward pass is a residual stream: xl+1 = xl + Attn(xl) + MLP(xl + Attn), per layer, with all positions processed in parallel. Early layers mostly encode position and syntax, middle layers resolve reference, and late layers align the stream with the unembedding so the final logits favour the answer. The bars above show 16 of the thousands of numbers in that stream.

7 · Parsing — how your question is read

When you type a question, the model has to work out what you're asking before it can answer — but nobody gave it a grammar book. There is no dictionary of nouns and verbs inside, and no code that draws sentence diagrams. The reading comprehension you've seen building up — tokens (lesson 1), attention links (lesson 4), layers specialising (lesson 6) — is the parser. Understanding assembles itself, layer by layer. Work through the three stages below and watch a question get taken apart.

Custom questions get a rough schematic parse — the presets are hand-annotated.

Schematic teaching annotations — the colours and links show the kind of structure attention has to build, not a readout of the real model's insides (it exposes none). Preset questions are annotated by hand; your own text gets simple word rules.

MediumThree things happen in sequence. Early layers settle surface structure — word order, which words group together. Middle layers wire the pointers: an asking-word like how many must find the thing being counted, a not must find the verb it cancels (the second preset is a trap of exactly this kind). Late layers hold the result as a working interpretation the answer is generated from. The invisible template tokens around your question (lesson 11's debug strip) also get parsed: the model distinguishes “the human is asking” from “text to continue”.

ExpertWhat linguists call parsing — constituency, dependency structure, semantic roles — shows up in probes of the residual stream: syntactic tree depth and dependency relations are linearly decodable from middle-layer representations, and individual attention heads track relations like subject→verb and pronoun→antecedent. None of it is symbolic: there is no tree data structure, only vectors that behave as if a tree were computed, well enough that next-token loss falls. Ambiguity is therefore a bet, not a lookup — with two valid parses, probability mass follows the training distribution, and the loser is simply never represented. That is why a misread question gets a fluent, confident, wrong answer: generation is faithful to the parse, and the parse was the error.

Reading it: each hidden node computes h = tanh(Σ w·x + b), each output word gets a score z = Σ h·W + b, and softmax turns scores into probabilities that add to 100%: p = ez / Σ ez. During training, the error for the correct word is loss = −log p(correct), and gradient descent nudges every weight against its gradient: w ← w − η · ∂loss/∂w, where the output gradient has the beautifully simple form p − 1 for the correct word and p for every wrong one — push down what it wrongly believed, push up what it missed, a fraction η at a time.

Train it on your own sentences

Replace its whole world: write a few short, repetitive sentences (one per line, at least two words each). The model rebuilds its vocabulary from your words and retrains from scratch. Patterns you repeat are the patterns it will learn.

Try this: before training, pick “cat” — the prediction is near-random.

Train, then look again: after “cat” it should strongly favour “sat”, and after “the” it should spread across “cat”, “dog” and “bird” — because that is exactly the pattern in its tiny world. You are watching lesson 3 (training) create lesson 5 (prediction), with nothing hidden.

MediumInside, each word goes in as numbers, passes through two hidden layers of 16 and 12 nodes, and comes out as probabilities for the next word. Node size and brightness in the 3D view above are real activations, and the amber lines trace the strongest connections behind the prediction — nothing is staged.

ExpertThe network, exactly: one-hot input → 16 tanh units → 12 tanh units → softmax over a 12-word vocabulary, trained by SGD on next-word pairs. Unlike the Llama in lesson 11, every weight and activation lives in this page, so the amber route can show the largest |h·W| contributions to the winning logit. It is an MLP, not a transformer — no attention — which is what makes total inspection possible.

9 · Context — why it forgets

The model can only remember a limited amount of your conversation. When too much has been said, the oldest words fall away and are forgotten — it can't miss them, because for the model they no longer exist. Add sentences and watch the beginning disappear.

0 / 18 tokens in window · 0 tokens forgotten

Just pushed out:

Context window fill

Real windows are far bigger (this small model advertises up to 128k tokens), but the rule is identical at any size. This is also why long chats “drift”: the instruction you gave at the start may literally have left the window.

MediumThat limited amount has a name: the context window — a fixed number of recent tokens. Your prompts and the model's answers all live in it together; past the limit, the oldest tokens drop off the front, exactly as in the demo above.

ExpertInference is stateless: the entire state is the token sequence inside that fixed window (the local model advertises 128k tokens; the toy window above holds 18). Tokens beyond the limit are not compressed or summarised — they are absent from the next forward pass, which is why long sessions silently lose early instructions.

Why it fails: counting needs letters, but the model sees tokens (lesson 1); citations are easy to imitate without existing; and the maths answer that “sounds right” (10 cents) out-competes the correct one (5 cents) unless the model slows down. Bigger models fall for these less — none are immune.

12 · Small vs premium — why scale makes it smarter

Everything you have done so far used a model with about a billion knobs, running on your own computer. Premium models — the big hosted ones, like muse-wonder — work exactly the same way: tokens in, embeddings, attention, next-token probabilities out. There is no secret extra ingredient in the recipe. The difference is scale, polish, and where it runs: vastly more knobs, trained on vastly more text, finished with extra training that teaches it to follow instructions and show its working.

Why would “more of the same” make something smarter? A bigger model has room to store more patterns, more facts, and more subtle relationships between them — and it has seen more examples of every kind of problem during training. Its next-token guesses (lesson 5) are simply right more often, especially on rare and complicated things. Switch the measure below and compare — the two blocks are drawn at true volume scale: the small cube is your whole model, and the big block's volume is the real multiple of it. A 200× gap sounds abstract; a block that swallows 200 of your model, drawn honestly, isn't. (The grid carved into the big block shows roughly how many small cubes fit inside.)

MediumThe pattern behind the towers is a scaling law: prediction loss falls smoothly and predictably as you increase parameters, data, and compute together — a straight line on a log-log plot, found empirically across many model families. Double all three and the model gets measurably better at next-token prediction, and downstream abilities (arithmetic, code, translation) ride along. There is also a right ratio: research (the “Chinchilla” result) found many models were under-trained for their size — roughly 20 training tokens per parameter is a better balance than a huge model fed little text.

ExpertThe arithmetic of scale: training compute ≈ 6 · parameters · training-tokens FLOPs, so the premium column multiplies two big numbers together — that is why frontier training runs are measured in megawatt-months. Inference costs roughly 2 · parameters FLOPs per generated token (before attention and memory traffic), which is why premium answers are metered and your 1B feels instant. Three caveats keep scale honest: (1) many premium systems are mixtures-of-experts — total parameters overstate the active ones used per token; (2) returns diminish — each halving of loss costs exponentially more compute, and high-quality text is finite (the “data wall”); (3) scale is not the whole gap — data curation, post-training (instruction tuning, RLHF/preference optimisation), longer deliberate generation, and tool use are engineered on top. Your local model is the same kind of machine with every dial turned down — and its weights rounded to 8-bit integers (Q8_0) to fit, which costs a little accuracy by itself.

13 · From predictor to assistant — the polish layers

Here is a puzzle this course has set up. Lesson 3 trained a machine that only predicts the next token. Lesson 11's model answers questions, follows instructions, formats replies, and stops when it's done. Prediction alone does none of that: a raw (“base”) model given “What is the capital of France?” happily continues with more quiz questions, because that's what question-shaped text usually looks like in the wild. The assistant behaviour is trained on afterwards, in stages. Click through the pipeline, then compare the same prompt before and after the polish.

Teaching reconstructions, written to show the typical difference — not recordings of a specific model. Your local Llama is an instruction-tuned model already; the base column is what its untuned sibling would tend to do with the same text.

MediumStage 2 (instruction tuning, or SFT) trains on thousands of examples of “instruction → good response”, so question-shaped input starts predicting answer-shaped output. Stage 3 ranks several candidate answers with human (or AI) preferences and nudges the model toward the winners — helpful, honest, harmless is the usual slogan. The chat template tokens from lesson 11's debug strip are part of this: they mark who is speaking, and the tuned model has learned that after the user's turn ends, an assistant's turn — an answer, not more questions — begins.

ExpertMechanically, stages 2–3 are still lesson 3: gradient descent on next-token loss. SFT is ordinary fine-tuning on curated demonstrations. Preference training classically fits a reward model to human rankings, then optimises the policy against it with RL (PPO) under a KL penalty to the SFT model; newer pipelines (DPO and relatives) skip the explicit reward model and optimise directly on chosen/rejected pairs. The KL leash matters: without it, reward hacking eats the model — it finds degenerate text the reward model over-scores. Known side-effects to watch for in the wild: sycophancy (agreeing with the user's premise), refusal styles that vary by topic, and an “alignment tax” on some raw capabilities. The system prompt is parsed like any other tokens (lesson 7) but sits earlier in the context and, in tuned models, carries trained-in priority: instruction hierarchy is a learned behaviour, not a hard access-control system — which is exactly why prompt injection (lesson 10's missing half) works at all.

14 · Finding answers — retrieval (RAG)

The model in lesson 11 knows only what training baked into its weights, and lesson 9's context window forgets. Real systems cheat, productively: before answering, they look things up. Documents are chopped into chunks, each chunk is turned into an embedding (lesson 2), and your question is embedded too. The chunks whose vectors sit closest to the question get pasted into the context window, and the model answers with the evidence in front of it. That's retrieval-augmented generation. Try it on a tiny fictional archive — the answer exists only inside these documents, so a model without retrieval would have to guess.

The similarity scores here are real arithmetic on this toy archive (word-overlap vectors, cosine distance — a miniature of lesson 2's geometry with learned embeddings swapped for counting). Real systems embed with the model itself, search millions of chunks with approximate nearest neighbours, and re-rank.

MediumNotice what retrieval does to the failure modes you know: the knowledge now lives outside the weights, so it can be updated, cited, and deleted without retraining; the context window (lesson 9) becomes working memory instead of the whole world; and hallucination (lesson 10) drops when the answer is copied from evidence — though it returns the moment the retriever fetches the wrong chunk confidently. Chunk size is a real dial: too small and the answer's other half is in the next chunk; too big and you pay tokens (lesson 12's compute) to paste mostly-irrelevant text.

ExpertProduction RAG is a pipeline, and each stage has its own metrics: chunking (fixed, recursive, or semantic boundaries; overlap to protect cross-boundary answers), embedding model choice (dimension, domain fit), index (exact cosine for small corpora, HNSW/IVF approximate search at scale, trading recall for latency), hybrid retrieval (dense vectors plus BM25 keyword match — pure semantics misses exact names and numbers), re-ranking with a cross-encoder, and finally generation conditioned on the retrieved set. Evaluation splits in two: retrieval quality (recall@k — was the gold chunk even pasted in?) versus generation faithfulness (did the answer stay inside the evidence?). Most “RAG doesn't work” reports are retrieval failures wearing generation's clothes. And the security footnote: pasted documents are tokens too — a poisoned chunk can carry instructions, which is prompt injection arriving by the front door.

15 · Capstone — take one answer apart

Everything you've learned, fired at once. This page walks you through dissecting a single live answer with the whole toolkit: predict first (the strongest learning habit in the course), parse the question, generate, inspect the candidates, and read the debug numbers like an engineer. Each step ticks itself off as you do it — here, or over in the lessons where it lives.

○
1 · Predict. The letter-count trap, one more time: will the local model count the r's in “strawberry” correctly?
○
2 · Parse it. Open the strawberry question in Lesson 7 and walk it to stage 3 — name the count target and the format constraint before the model sees it.
○
3 · Run it. Generate the trap answer in Lesson 11. The scoreboard judge from Lesson 10 grades it against your prediction automatically.
○

Capstone: 0 of 5 steps done.

When all five are ticked, you've used every instrument in the workshop on one answer: parsing, prediction, sampling, candidate inspection, and server debug. That loop — predict, run, dissect — is the whole skill this course was building.

MediumWhy predict first? Because watching an answer arrive teaches recognition, but committing to a guess first exposes your own model of the model — and the corrections stick. Every professional evaluation workflow is this loop at scale: form an expectation, run, compare, and let the gap between the two rewrite your understanding.

ExpertWhat you just did by hand is, formally, a one-item behavioural evaluation with a pre-registered hypothesis — the same shape as a benchmark, minus the statistics. Scale it and the open problems appear: judge reliability (your Lesson 10 heuristic can misjudge, exactly like an LLM judge), contamination (items the model may have memorised), and variance across seeds and temperatures (lesson 5). Hold those three in mind and you can read any leaderboard like an engineer instead of a fan.
